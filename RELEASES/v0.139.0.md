# v0.139.0 — the boundaries the government already drew

Two tools, one module that reads four file formats and inverts four map
projections, seven columns on Field, one button on the block form, and two
whitelisted methods behind it.

---

## The one idea

A farm's field boundaries already exist.

The Farm Service Agency drew them. They are what the farm's acreage report, its
crop insurance and every ARC/PLC, CRP and disaster payment are measured against,
and the grower can walk into the county office and be handed the lot on a memory
stick. Tracing those same shapes by eye off a satellite image — which is what
everybody does when importing is hard — produces a **second set of the farm's own
boundaries** that disagrees with the first by a few percent.

Only one of the two is the one FSA pays on.

```
  before                             after
  ──────────────────────────────     ──────────────────────────────
  office hands over clu.zip          office hands over clu.zip
    → nothing on this site reads       → read_fsa_clu_file says what
      a shapefile                        is in it
    → trace 40 blocks by eye           → import_fsa_clu_boundaries
      off imagery                        says which block each CLU is
    → two versions of the farm's       → apply, and the polygon FSA
      boundaries, neither filed          measures on is the one on
                                          the record
```

---

## Why `import_field_boundary_geojson` could not do this

It has existed since v0.31.0 and takes a FeatureCollection whose every Feature
carries `properties.field_name` and `properties.parcel_hint`.

**A CLU carries neither.** It carries a farm number, a tract number, a field
number within that tract, a GUID and FSA's own calculated acreage — and the
grower calls the block "the top of Dry Hollow". A file that is entirely correct
therefore matches nothing at all through that door, and the report comes back
saying every field is unregistered, which reads like the farm's data being wrong.

So the matching in `import_fsa_clu_boundaries` is on **what a CLU actually has**,
in the order of how much the identifier is worth:

| | matched against | why it is where it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `fsa_clu_identifier` | The CLU GUID is the only identifier FSA guarantees. It is what makes next year's file an UPDATE rather than a second copy of the farm. |
| 2 | `fsa_tract_number` + `fsa_clu_number` | What the office says out loud, and what every acreage report is filed under. Survives a reissued GUID. |
| 3 | `block_number`, within a parcel | A farm that numbered its blocks the way FSA numbers its fields matches here on the FIRST import, before anything carries an FSA column at all. Compared after normalising, so block `"03"` and field 3 are one block. |
| 4 | `field_name`, within a parcel | For a farm already using `T1234-3`. |

The report says which of the four each match used. Forty by `clu_identifier` on
the second run is the pair working; forty by `block_number` again means something
renamed itself.

**It never creates a block unless asked.** A CLU matching nothing is reported and
skipped; `create_missing=true` registers it as `T<tract>-<field>` under its
parcel with FSA's own acreage. A boundary landing on the wrong block is a spray
record pointing at the wrong ground, and a farm handed forty invented blocks will
not notice the two that were right to invent.

## The four things the office actually hands out

`erpnext_mcp/fsa.py` reads all four, and none of them through a new dependency:

* **A zipped shapefile set** — `.shp` geometry, `.dbf` attributes, `.shx` index
  nothing here needs, `.prj` coordinate system. The common case.
* **A bare `.shp` with its sidecars** — refused with the sentence that says to zip
  the set, because a `.shp` alone has neither the tract numbers nor the
  coordinate system.
* **KML or KMZ** — what comes back from the crop-reporting side and from anybody
  who exported "for Google Earth".
* **GeoJSON** — from a grower whose agronomist already converted it.

`pyshp` would read the first and `pyproj` would reproject it, and neither is a
dependency of this app. The shapefile format is a frozen 1998 specification about
a page and a half long for the polygon case, and dBase III is older than that.
Both are read in a few hundred lines that cannot break on an upgrade, because
neither format will ever change again.

## Reprojection is the part that actually matters

FSA's own distribution is geographic NAD83 — degrees, which is what GeoJSON
wants. A set that has been through a vendor's system comes back in whatever that
system projects to, and in US agriculture that is one of four things:

| projection | where it turns up |
|---|---|
| **Albers Equal Area** | EPSG:5070. The FSA/NRCS house projection. |
| **Transverse Mercator** | every UTM zone. |
| **Lambert Conformal Conic** | most State Plane zones — in metres or in US survey feet. |
| **Web Mercator** | anything that has been through a web map. |

Those coordinates are **metres**. A State Plane easting is around 2,000,000, and
handing that to a GeoJSON reader produces a polygon "not on Earth" — a true error
message and a useless one.

All four are inverted here from the closed forms in USGS Professional Paper 1395
(Snyder, 1987), against the ellipsoid the file's **own `.prj`** names. Checked
against `pyproj` over a grid of points in five zones during development: **exact
to the fourth decimal of a millimetre** on plain NAD83 zones, and the residual
1.27 m on the HARN ones is `pyproj` applying the WGS84↔NAD83(HARN) datum shift
this module deliberately does not.

**Anything else is refused by name.** "This is an Oblique Mercator, which I
cannot un-project — re-export as EPSG:4326" is a sentence somebody can act on. A
boundary transformed by a guess is worse than one that was never transformed.

### The failure with no symptom

A file of metres read as degrees is refused loudly. A file of **US survey feet**
whose false easting is subtracted in **metres** produces a perfectly valid,
perfectly closed polygon about 1,100 km from the farm. The false origin is
converted with the coordinates, once, in `transformer`; `test_fsa_clu.py` has a
test for that case specifically, and it fails when that conversion is removed.

## Which ring is a hole

A shapefile record is a **flat list of rings** with nothing saying which is a
field and which is a hole in one. The specification's answer is winding — outer
clockwise, hole counter-clockwise — and half the tools that write shapefiles do
not enforce it.

Get it wrong and a ten-acre field with a wetland exclusion comes back as two
polygons totalling twelve acres. **Nobody would question that number.**

So nesting is decided by **containment and not by winding**: a ring inside an odd
number of other rings is a hole in the smallest one containing it, a ring inside
an even number is a field in its own right, and the RFC 7946 winding is imposed
on the way out. There are tests in both windings, and one for the two-rings-and-no
-hole case — a CLU split by a county road is one field in two pieces, and
subtracting the second from the first would remove ten real acres.

## The column names are not stable and never were

The 2008 public release spelled them `CLUID`, `TRACTNBR`, `CLUNBR`, `CALCACRES`.
An office export today may spell the same four `CLU_IDENTIFIER`, `TRACT_NUMBER`,
`CLU_NUMBER`, `CALCULATED_ACRES`; a vendor round-trip renames them again and
dBase truncates anything past ten characters while it is at it.

Every spelling is looked up on one **squashed** key — upper-cased with every
separator removed — so `TRACT_NBR`, `tract nbr` and `TractNbr` are one column
rather than three. A column this app has never heard of is kept verbatim: it is
somebody's own note about their own ground.

And `1234.0` out of a dBase numeric column, `"0012"` out of a text one and `12`
out of JSON are **one tract**. If those do not normalise to one string then next
season's file matches nothing and creates a second copy of every field — which is
not an error, it is forty new blocks that look right.

## What goes on the record

Seven columns on Field, in a new collapsible **FSA Farm Records** section:

| column | |
|---|---|
| `fsa_farm_number` | The farm number the block is administered under. |
| `fsa_tract_number` | The tract. A standard filter, because it is what the office asks for. |
| `fsa_clu_number` | The field number within the tract. |
| `fsa_clu_identifier` | FSA's GUID. **Not unique here on purpose** — a farm that splits one CLU into two blocks it manages differently is a normal thing to do, and both carry it honestly. |
| `fsa_calc_acres` | FSA's acreage, exactly as the file gave it. |
| `fsa_hel_type` | The highly-erodible classification. Conservation compliance on a HEL field is a condition of every program payment. |
| `fsa_import_date` | When this block last took a boundary from a CLU file. FSA revises CLUs. |

**FSA's acreage is kept and never computed with.** `area_computed_acres`,
the centroid, the bounding box and the H3 coverage all come from the polygon, as
they have since v0.12.0. The two figures routinely differ by a percent; a
register that showed one of them would be hiding the disagreement rather than
settling it, so `get_field` and `list_fields` report both — under an `fsa` key
that appears **only on blocks that have one**, so the majority of farms that have
never imported a CLU file do not get seven nulls on every row of every register.

Every check `set_field_boundary` makes still applies, **per CLU**: the polygon is
parsed, self-intersection is refused, and a shape enclosing more than 25% away
from the block's recorded acreage is refused rather than saved. One bad CLU in
forty is a bad CLU — it is named and the other thirty-nine still apply.

## The button, and why it is on the block form and on no other

`parcel_map.js` has said since v0.33.0 that there is **no county import on the
block form**, because a county publishes TAX LOTS and a tax lot is what a Parcel
is — a county button there would offer to set a block's boundary to the whole lot
it sits in.

v0.139.0 is the exact mirror of that argument rather than an exception to it. **A
CLU *is* a field**, drawn at the resolution the ground is actually farmed at. On
a Parcel form it would be the wrong unit in the other direction: a tract holds
several CLUs and crosses tax lots.

"Import from FSA (CLU)" sits beside Save Boundary on the Field form. It reads the
file **in the browser** and posts it as base64: nothing is stored, so a grower who
picked the wrong export off the memory stick has left nothing behind. Every CLU in
the file is drawn dashed on the satellite image and picking one applies its shape
**and its identifiers** to this block. Ticking "match the whole file" instead runs
the bulk import — the plan first, in a list, and the write only on a second press.

Two whitelisted methods behind it, both gated on **write permission on Field**
and neither inheriting anything: `api/gis.py` now exports four, enumerated by
`test_gis_import.TheSaveSurfaceIsClosed` so a fifth arriving is a deliberate edit.

## Interop, kept rather than claimed

`read_fsa_clu_file(as_feature_collection=true)` returns the file as a GeoJSON
FeatureCollection with `field_name` and `parcel_hint` on every Feature — the
shape `import_field_boundary_geojson` takes — with the FSA columns alongside under
`fsa_` keys. There is a test that feeds that output straight into that tool and
asserts what it set, because a translation nobody exercises is a translation that
is wrong.

It is the lesser path and says so: that tool matches on the name alone and knows
nothing about CLU identifiers, so it will not recognise a block on a re-import.
